Overview

Vanilla RNN, GRU, and LSTM compared on the International Airline Passengers dataset (144 monthly counts, 1949–1960). A baseline (Test 1) vs. an improved training regime (Test 2) shows that training methodology — not just architecture — determines whether a theoretically stronger model delivers better results. Best result: LSTM RMSE = 33.17 (Test 2, lr=0.005).

Method

Figure 1: Shared recurrent network architecture — 64 hidden units, dropout, FC output

All three models share the same structure: a recurrent layer with 64 hidden units, a dropout layer (rate=0.0), and a fully connected output layer producing a single scalar. The vanilla RNN uses tanh activation; GRU and LSTM use gating mechanisms.

Dataset: 144 monthly passenger counts normalized to [0,1] with min-max scaling. Sliding window of length 12 (matching the seasonal period) creates input-output pairs. 80/20 temporal split: 105 training sequences, 27 validation sequences.

In Test 2, RMSE improves steadily from 16 to 64 hidden units, then worsens at 128 due to overfitting on just 105 training sequences. 64 units provides the right capacity for this small dataset.

Conclusion

  • In Test 1 (baseline), the LSTM performed worst (RMSE=69.64) despite its architectural advantage — a fixed learning rate fails to navigate its complex loss landscape.
  • In Test 2 (adaptive scheduler + extended training + checkpointing), the LSTM recovered to RMSE=33.17 — the best result overall and a 52% improvement over its own baseline.
  • Architecture alone does not determine performance; a more powerful model requires a training regime capable of exploiting its capacity.
  • Optimal configuration: LSTM, lr=0.005, 64 hidden units, 300 epochs, plateau-based LR scheduler.
